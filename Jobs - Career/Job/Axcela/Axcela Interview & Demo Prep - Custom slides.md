## 1. Demo Lesson Script (20 minutes, STT-maximized) - Custom

Your material is pitched at B2, but the interviewer said the students are **B1**. Say this openly in your intro to the demo — it shows awareness, not a mistake: _"I know this deck is labeled B2, but since our learners today are B1, I'll simplify my language and slow down on the modal + perfect passive forms, which are the trickiest part for this level."_ That one line alone signals strong lesson awareness to an interviewer.

Because you only have 15–30 minutes, **do not try to teach the whole PPT.** Cut it down to three stages that showcase the most teaching skill: a fast lead-in, a discovery-based grammar stage (Test-Teach-Test), and a speaking stage. Skip the reading text and MCQ cloze entirely — they're recognition tasks, low on STT, and won't showcase you well in a short slot.

### Timing overview

|Stage|Time|STT focus|
|---|---|---|
|Lead-in / Warm-up|3 min|Pair discussion|
|Grammar: Discovery + CCQs|9 min|Pair correction + eliciting|
|Speaking: Picture comparison|6 min|Long turn + peer feedback|
|Wrap-up|2 min|Student self-report|

---

### STAGE 1 — Lead-in (3 min)

**Goal:** Activate the topic, get students talking in the first 30 seconds. Minimal TTT.

**Script:**

> "Hi everyone! Quick question before we start — [nominate a name], have you or anyone you know ever had a medical emergency? Just a word or two is fine." _(Take 1–2 short answers, 20 seconds max — don't let this become a full discussion, it's just a hook.)_ "Great. Today we're going into the world of clinics and emergencies — and along the way, we'll upgrade your passive voice to a much more advanced, professional level. Let's dive in."

**STT tactic:** Ask the question, then go silent. Resist the urge to fill the pause — count to 5 in your head before re-nominating. Silence is uncomfortable for new teachers but it's where STT lives.

---

### STAGE 2 — Grammar: "Fix the Voice" Discovery Task (9 min)

This is a **Test-Teach-Test** approach — students attempt the language _before_ you explain the rule. It's the strongest ELT-methodology signal you can give in a short demo, so lean into it.

**Script:**

> "I'm going to show you 4 sentences. Some are correct, some have a mistake. In pairs, you have 90 seconds — decide which are correct, and fix the ones that aren't. Don't worry about being right, just try."

_(Show/say these 4 — a representative sample from the deck, not all 10):_

1. The proposal could written by the assistant. _(incorrect)_
2. The meeting has been scheduled for Friday. _(correct)_
3. The documents should have sent yesterday. _(incorrect)_
4. He got fire after missing several deadlines. _(incorrect)_

> "ICQ: Are you working alone or with a partner?" → _With a partner._ "ICQ: How much time do you have?" → _90 seconds._

_(Set a visible/mental timer. Go silent. Monitor by listening only — do not interrupt or correct in real time.)_

**Elicit answers (don't lecture):**

> "OK, time's up! Pair A, what did you decide about sentence 1?" _(Nominate 2–3 different pairs for different sentences. Write corrected forms on screen/whiteboard as they say them, in their words where possible.)_

**Concept-check the trickiest structure — Perfect Modal Passive** using target sentence: _"The documents should have been sent yesterday."_

> **CCQ 1:** "Were the documents sent yesterday?" → **No.** **CCQ 2:** "Are we talking about now, or about the past?" → **The past — something that should have happened but didn't.**

_(These two CCQs are gold for an interviewer to hear — they isolate meaning without using grammar terminology, which is exactly what CELTA/TESOL assessors look for.)_

**Mini-clarify (only after eliciting, keep to 30 seconds):**

> "So notice the pattern: modal + have + been + past participle. This is how we talk about things that _should have_ happened but didn't — really useful for giving feedback at work without sounding harsh. For example: 'The report should have been finished' sounds much softer than 'You didn't finish the report.'"

**STT tactic:** You spoke for roughly 20% of this stage; students did the rest — pair talk, negotiation, and reporting back.

---

### STAGE 3 — Speaking: Picture Comparison (6 min)

**Goal:** Freer, personalized production — the highest-value STT stage in the whole lesson, and the one interviewers weight most heavily.

**Script:**

> "Now, look at these two photos — Photo 1, a patient being examined at a clinic. Photo 2, a person getting home care treatment. In pairs, you'll compare them. Try to use at least one passive sentence like the ones we just practiced."

_(Model just ONE sentence yourself, then get out of the way):_

> "For example, I might say: 'In the first photo, the patient is being checked up on by a doctor.' Now you try — you have 2 minutes."

**ICQ:** "Are you describing one photo or comparing both?" → _Comparing both._

_(Monitor silently, note 2–3 errors for delayed correction — never interrupt mid-turn.)_

**Delayed error correction (after, on screen, anonymously):**

> "I heard some great passive sentences! Let's look at one I want us to upgrade together: 'The doctor is checking the patient.' How could we make this passive?" _(elicit: "The patient is being checked by the doctor.")_

---

### STAGE 4 — Wrap-up (2 min)

> "To finish — in one sentence each, tell me: what's one passive structure you'll try to use this week, at work or in daily life?" _(Nominate 2–3 students quickly. This closes on STT, not on you summarizing.)_

**Closing line to the interviewer (drop character for 10 seconds):**

> "That's roughly how I'd run this stage in a real class — I focused on eliciting rather than explaining, used CCQs to check meaning instead of just asking 'do you understand,' and kept teacher talk under a third of stage time throughout."

## 3. Top 3 Hardest Behavioral Questions (Adult Learners) — High-Impact Answers

### Q1: "How do you handle a student who seems unmotivated or tired, especially in an evening class after work?"

- **Acknowledge, don't ignore:** Open by naming it lightly — _"Long day? Let's ease in"_ — rather than pretending energy that isn't there.
- **Lower the stakes first 5 minutes:** Start with a light, low-pressure warm-up (opinion question, quick pair chat) before grammar — builds momentum before demanding output.
- **Tie language to their world, immediately:** Use examples from their actual job/industry so the lesson feels relevant, not academic.
- **Give visible small wins:** Praise specific language use ("nice use of the passive there") — adults respond to competence-based motivation, not gold stars.
- **Vary interaction pattern:** Switch pairs/groups every 10–15 minutes to reset energy without adding content load.

### Q2: "A student frequently misses classes or is late due to work — how do you keep them progressing?"

- **Build a lesson structure that survives gaps:** Each session should stand alone thematically (like this passive voice lesson) so missing one class doesn't break continuity.
- **Quick catch-up ritual:** First 2 minutes of any class = a 1-sentence recap from a peer, not a re-teach from you — keeps pace for the group.
- **Send a 1-line follow-up:** A short recap message or key phrase after class costs little time but keeps absent students anchored.
- **Reframe consistency, not attendance, as the goal:** Track their language use over time rather than penalizing gaps — adults respond better to progress-tracking than attendance-shaming.
- **Ask directly, once:** A brief private check-in ("what's realistic for you this month?") often surfaces a schedule fix neither of you would've guessed.

### Q3: "How do you correct a professional adult's grammar mistakes without embarrassing them, especially in front of peers?"

- **Default to delayed, anonymous correction:** Collect errors while monitoring, put them on screen/board _after_ the activity with no names attached.
- **Correct output, not the person:** Frame as "let's upgrade this sentence together" rather than "you made a mistake."
- **Elicit the fix, don't supply it:** Ask "how could we make this more natural?" — lets the student self-correct and save face.
- **Match correction to the goal of the activity:** In a fluency/speaking stage, let errors pass and note them for later; only correct in real time during accuracy-focused stages.
- **Normalize it explicitly once, early:** A short line like _"I'll note a few things as we go and we'll fix them together at the end — that's completely normal, even at advanced levels"_ removes the fear before it starts.

## 4. Acronyms used in the prep package
**Teaching methodology terms**

- **STT — Student Talking Time.** The amount of class time students spend actually speaking/producing language, as opposed to listening to the teacher. Maximizing STT is a core ELT priority because students learn by _using_ language, not by hearing about it.
- **TTT — Teacher Talking Time.** The amount of class time the teacher spends speaking (giving instructions, explaining, lecturing). Good practice generally means keeping TTT low and STT high — every minute you talk is a minute a student isn't practicing.
- **CCQ — Concept Checking Question.** A question used to confirm students understand the _meaning_ of a target structure, without relying on grammar terminology or a simple "Do you understand?" (which students often answer "yes" to even when they don't). E.g., "Were the documents sent yesterday?" checks understanding of "should have been sent" without naming the grammar.
- **ICQ — Instruction Checking Question.** A question used to confirm students understood the _task instructions_ before they start (e.g., "Are you working alone or with a partner?" / "How much time do you have?"). Prevents wasted time from students doing the wrong thing.

**Qualifications / frameworks**

- **CELTA — Certificate in English Language Teaching to Adults.** A widely recognized, Cambridge-administered TEFL/TESOL qualification, generally considered the industry-standard entry credential for English teachers, especially in adult and exam-prep contexts.
- **TESOL — Teaching English to Speakers of Other Languages.** A broader umbrella term for the field/qualification type; used somewhat interchangeably with CELTA/TEFL depending on the certifying body.
- **ELT — English Language Teaching.** General field/industry term.

**Exam / level references**

- **FCE — First Certificate in English** (also called Cambridge B2 First). A Cambridge English exam at B2 level; your source lesson material was designed with FCE-style tasks (multiple choice cloze, open cloze, key word transformation) in mind, which is why those question formats appear in the deck.
- **B1 / B2 — CEFR levels** (Common European Framework of Reference for Languages). B1 = intermediate, B2 = upper-intermediate. This is the mismatch I flagged earlier: your lesson content is pitched at B2, but Axcela's students are B1.
