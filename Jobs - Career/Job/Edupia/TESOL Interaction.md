## 1. Maximize ICQs: make instructions almost impossible to misunderstand

**ICQ = Instruction Checking Question.**

Don't ask:

> “Do you understand?”

A child can say “yes” without understanding.

Instead, check the **action** they need to perform.

### Example: vocabulary matching

Teacher:

> “Look at the pictures. Match each picture with the correct word.”

Then:

- “Do you write or speak?” → **Write.**
    
- “One word or two words?” → **One word.**
    
- “Do you match the picture with the word?” → **Yes.**
    
- “Which number do you start with?” → **Number 1.**
    

Then immediately:

> “Ready? Go!”

### The ICQ formula

For almost every activity, ask:

**WHAT? + HOW? + WHERE? + HOW MANY?**

|Activity|ICQs|
|---|---|
|Read|“Do you read or listen?”|
|Listen|“Do you speak while I play?” → No|
|Match|“Do you match words or pictures?”|
|Circle|“Do you circle or underline?”|
|Draw|“Do you draw one thing or three things?”|
|Pair work|“Do you work alone or together?”|
|Speaking|“Do you answer or ask?”|
|Writing|“How many sentences?”|
|Game|“Who goes first?”|

**Important:** Don't turn ICQs into a quiz. Usually **1–3 short ICQs** are enough.

# 2. Maximize CCQs: check meaning, not vocabulary recognition

**CCQ = Concept Checking Question.**

The biggest mistake is:

> “Do you understand ‘hungry’?”

Instead, create a situation where the child demonstrates the meaning.

### Example: “hungry”

Teacher:

> “I didn't eat breakfast. My stomach is empty. Am I hungry?”

Student:

> “Yes!”

Teacher:

> “Do I want food?”

> “Yes!”

Teacher:

> “Am I hungry or sleepy?”

> “Hungry!”

That's a genuine meaning check.

### CCQ types for K–12

Use these constantly:

**Either/or**

> “Is it big or small?”

**Yes/no**

> “Can a fish fly?”

**True/false**

> “If I'm freezing, am I hot?”

**Situation**

> “You studied all night. Are you tired?”

**Contrast**

> “Is a puppy bigger or smaller than an elephant?”

**Time**

> “Did this happen yesterday or tomorrow?”

**Function**

> “Do we use a spoon to eat soup or write?”

# 3. Use TPR as a meaning anchor

TPR (**Total Physical Response**) works especially well with younger learners because you're giving them a physical representation of language.

Don't just say:

> “Stand up.”

Build a **gesture-language connection**.

For example:

> “Stand up!” ↑  
> “Sit down!” ↓  
> “Jump!”  
> “Touch your nose!” 👃  
> “Turn around!” 🔄  
> “Clap!” 👏

Then gradually remove your demonstration.

### The progression

**Stage 1 — Teacher models**

> “Touch your head.”  
> _Teacher touches head._

**Stage 2 — Teacher + student**

> “Touch your head!”

**Stage 3 — Teacher speaks only**

> “Touch your head.”

**Stage 4 — Student responds independently**

> “What do we do?”

Student:

> “Touch your head!”

Now you've moved from **TPR → comprehension → production**.

# 4. Combine all three: ICQ + CCQ + TPR

This is where you can make your ClassIn lesson much stronger.

### Example: teaching “behind”

Put a picture on the ClassIn board.

🐱 **behind the box** 📦

First, TPR/visual:

> “Behind.”

Move your hand behind the object.

Then CCQ:

> “Can I see the cat?”

> “No.”

> “Is the cat in front of the box?”

> “No.”

> “Is the cat behind the box?”

> “Yes.”

Then production:

> “Where is the cat?”

Student:

> “It's behind the box.”

Then TPR:

> “Put your pencil behind your book.”

Now you've checked:

**meaning → comprehension → physical response → language production.**

# 7. Don't overuse CCQs

A common TESOL mistake is turning every vocabulary item into an interrogation.

You don't need:

> “Is a banana food?”  
> “Can you eat it?”  
> “Is it a fruit?”  
> “Is it yellow?”  
> “Is it big?”

That's too much.

Use **the minimum number of questions necessary to establish meaning**.

For a young learner:

> “Can you eat it?” → Yes  
> “Is it a fruit or an animal?” → Fruit

Done.

Then **use the word**.

# 8. Make your TPR progressively harder

Don't keep TPR at the “Simon Says” level forever.

### Level 1 — Copy

> “Touch your head.”

### Level 2 — Listen

> “Touch your knees.”

No demonstration.

### Level 3 — Discriminate

> “Touch your head if I say _head_. Touch your knees if I say _knees_.”

### Level 4 — Understand sentences

> “Touch your head before you touch your knees.”

### Level 5 — Student becomes teacher

> “Give me an instruction.”

Student:

> “Touch your nose!”

Teacher performs it.

This final stage is excellent because **the student has to produce language**.

# 9. Use “student-as-teacher” to maximize speaking

This is especially useful when your class has limited speaking time.

Instead of:

> Teacher: “What's this?”  
> Student: “A pencil.”

Try:

> Teacher: “What's this?”  
> Student: “It's a pencil.”  
> Teacher: “Now you ask me.”  
> Student: “What's this?”  
> Teacher: “It's a ruler.”

Then:

> “Now ask me three questions.”

You've doubled the student's productive language without needing a new activity.

# 11. The “70/30” principle

For K–12 tutoring, aim to make the **student's learning activity** dominate the lesson rather than teacher talk.

A useful target is approximately:

> **Teacher talk: 30%**  
> **Student action/talk: 70%**

Don't obsess over the exact percentage. The important question is:

> **“Who is doing the work right now?”**

If you have been speaking for 90 seconds, that's usually a signal to make the student **say, point, move, choose, write, draw, or demonstrate something.**
