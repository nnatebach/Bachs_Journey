Children’s books/audiobooks are a great addition, especially since you’re focused on listening — native voices, natural rhythm, simple enough vocabulary that you’re building comprehension instead of just decoding. Here’s a leveled list.

### True beginner (A1) — short, highly repetitive

- **Petit Ours Brun** series — extremely simple sentences, huge picture support, made for actual toddlers so vocabulary is dead simple. Great for absolute first exposure.
- **T’choupi** series — similarly simple, everyday-life vocabulary (meals, bedtime, school)
- **La chenille qui fait des trous** (_The Very Hungry Caterpillar_, French edition) — if you know the English original, the repetition and predictability make it easy to follow even with minimal vocab

### Early beginner (A1–A2)

- **Le Petit Nicolas** (Sempé/Goscinny) — technically written for kids but genuinely enjoyable for adults too; short chapters, everyday school-boy vocabulary, mild humor. Audiobook narration is usually clear and well-paced.
- **Les Belles Histoires** (magazine-style stories, often available as audio) — varied simple stories, good for building listening stamina in short bursts

### Beginner–low intermediate (A2–B1)

- **Le Petit Prince** (_The Little Prince_) — the classic choice for learners; vocabulary is more sophisticated than it looks, but sentence structures are clean and the audiobook narration (many versions exist) is usually slow and deliberate. Don’t attempt this until solid A2 — the abstract/poetic language trips people up if you go too early.
- **Contes de Perrault** (Cinderella, Puss in Boots, etc., in original French) — fairy tale structure means you often already know the plot, which frees up mental bandwidth to focus on the language itself rather than following the story

### Intermediate (B1–B2)

- **Astérix** (as audiobook/BD companion) — richer vocabulary, wordplay, and cultural references; good bridge toward more adult content while staying fun
- **Harry Potter à l’école des sorciers** (French translation) — if you already know the story well in English, following the French audiobook is much easier since you’re not guessing plot, just mapping language

### Practical tip for listening specifically

Get the **audiobook + physical/ebook text together** at every level and read along the first time through — this is one of the highest-value techniques for building the sound-to-spelling connection, especially given how irregular French spelling is. Once a book feels easy to read-along, re-listen to it audio-only as pure listening practice — that repetition at increasing difficulty is exactly the kind of graded exposure that builds real comprehension, not just recognition.

Où trouver ces livres: Audible French catalog, or free public-domain audio for older classics (Le Petit Prince, Perrault) via LibriVox or YouTube.
