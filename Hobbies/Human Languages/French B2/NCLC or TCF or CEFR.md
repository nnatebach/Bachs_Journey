This is an important distinction because these are **not competing certifications**—they serve different purposes.

|System|What it is|Used for|"Stronger"?|
|---|---|---|---|
|**CEFR**|A language proficiency framework (A1–C2)|Schools, universities, employers worldwide|Best for describing your overall French level|
|**TCF Canada**|An official French language test|Canadian immigration|Required if you choose this test for PR|
|**NCLC**|The Canadian language benchmark (Levels 1–12)|IRCC uses it to assess immigration points|The standard Canada actually evaluates|

### Think of it this way

- **CEFR** is like a **grading scale** (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2).
- **TCF Canada** is the **exam** you take.
- **NCLC** is how the **Canadian government interprets your TCF Canada results**.

So if you take **TCF Canada**, you don't receive an NCLC certificate. You receive **TCF Canada scores**, and **IRCC converts those scores into NCLC levels** for immigration purposes.

## Which one matters for Canadian PR?

For immigration:

> **NCLC is the one that matters.**

IRCC awards eligibility and points based on your **NCLC level**, not your CEFR level.

For example:

- You study until you're around **B2 (CEFR)**.
- You take **TCF Canada**.
- Your results are converted to **NCLC 7, 8, 9, etc.**
- IRCC uses those NCLC levels to calculate your application.

## Which level should you aim for?

For your goal, I'd suggest:

- **Learning target:** **B2 CEFR**
- **Exam:** **TCF Canada**
- **Immigration target:** **NCLC 7 or higher in all four skills**

If you can achieve **NCLC 9 or above**, that's even better because higher language levels generally earn more points in systems like Express Entry. The exact benefit depends on your overall profile (age, education, work experience, English results, etc.).

### Your roadmap in one sentence

**Study to a solid B2 (CEFR), take TCF Canada, and aim for NCLC 7–9 or higher in every skill.**

That keeps your learning, your exam preparation, and your immigration objective aligned.
